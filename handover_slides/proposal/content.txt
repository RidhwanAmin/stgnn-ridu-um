ABSTRACT

Atrial ectopy burden predicts atrial fibrillation (AF) after ischaemic stroke and helps decide who receives prolonged rhythm monitoring, yet every threshold was derived on Holter electrocardiography (ECG), the scarce resource being allocated. Smartwatches report beat intervals with a per-beat quality flag, but the error of a derived daily ectopic-beat count is unknown, and their analysable data are concentrated at night while premature atrial contractions (PACs) peak in the afternoon. The aim is to establish whether smartwatch beat intervals, analysed with an estimator that models signal quality and unobserved time, can yield a daily atrial ectopy estimate whose error is characterised against ECG labels under simulated smartwatch losses and whose coverage after stroke is adequate, as a step towards comparison with ECG-derived thresholds. The objectives are to investigate the ectopy information lost when a photoplethysmographic (PPG) waveform is reduced to filtered beat intervals (RO1); to develop a two-scale spatio-temporal graph neural network (ST-GNN) that uses the quality flag and unobserved time as inputs (RO2); to evaluate the deployability of a 7-day smartwatch protocol in post-stroke survivors (RO3); and to evaluate and validate substituting a wearable estimate for the ECG count, by how often it reclassifies excessive supraventricular ectopic activity (ESVEA) and how wearable-based selections agree with clinical scores (RO4). The design combines the Pulsewatch and Icentia11k datasets with a prospective cohort of about 40 adults after ischaemic stroke or transient ischaemic attack. In pilot work on 33 held-out subjects from four PPG cohorts, a beat-graph classifier ranked AF above other rhythms well (AUROC 0.995 or higher where non-AF windows existed, one AF subject per cohort), but AF recall at a threshold fixed on validation subjects ranged from 45% to 100% across devices, and in 298 Icentia11k patients a 20% deviation filter removed 60.9% of PAC coupling intervals against 8.8% of normal intervals. The expected contribution is, to our knowledge, the first coverage-aware estimator of daily atrial ectopy from smartwatch intervals, with its error characterised under simulated device losses.

**Keywords:** atrial ectopy; photoplethysmography; graph neural network; informative missingness; stroke; wearable monitoring

# CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Background of the Study

Excessive supraventricular ectopic activity (ESVEA) marks a remodelled atrium. Binici et al. (2010) defined it as 30 or more supraventricular ectopic complexes per hour, or any run of 20 or more, on ambulatory ECG; stroke cohorts have since used 480 or more supraventricular beats per day, or runs of 10 to 29 seconds (Haeusler et al., 2025). In a meta-analysis of 20 studies, ESVEA was associated with later AF in patients after ischaemic stroke (hazard ratio [HR] 2.91; Yang et al., 2022), and with ischaemic stroke beyond AF itself (HR 1.96; Larsen et al., 2015), but the same meta-analysis found no significant association between ESVEA and recurrent ischaemic stroke or transient ischaemic attack (TIA) (HR 1.24, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.91 to 1.67). Ectopy is therefore treated as a selector for monitoring, not a recurrence predictor or an indication for anticoagulation (Schnabel et al., 2019).

In MonDAFIS, Holter recordings in 1,435 patients with acute ischaemic stroke found ESVEA in 25.3% but AF in 3.3%; by 24 months, new AF had been detected in 18.5% of patients with ESVEA against 5.9% without (Haeusler et al., 2025). Detecting AF moves a patient from antiplatelet therapy to anticoagulation, with a larger absolute benefit after stroke or TIA (McIntyre et al., 2024; Shoamanesh et al., 2025).

Prolonged monitoring finds more AF, from 5.1% with 72 hours or less to 29.15% with three months (Dussault et al., 2015) and 34% beyond 24 months with implantable monitors (Tsivgoulis et al., 2019), but it is limited. Two routes allocate it. The first is ectopy burden: the 90-day probability of AF rises from under 9% in patients with fewer than 100 atrial premature beats per 24 hours to 40% beyond 1,500 (Gladstone et al., 2015). The second is clinical risk scores, which need no monitoring but discriminate inconsistently, with areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) from 0.54 to 0.94 across cohorts (Kishore et al., 2022) and no model excellent on external validation (Helbitz et al., 2025). One day of ECG is insufficient: first-day sensitivity for ESVEA was 44.6% (Måneheim et al., 2023).

A smartwatch could observe ectopy repeatedly without consuming Holter capacity. Smartwatches already detect AF well at window level (pooled sensitivity 95%, specificity 97%; Barrera et al., 2025). Their coverage, however, is partial: stroke survivors wore the watch on 21.2 of 30 days (Ding et al., 2023), and 22.3% of PPG minutes were unusable against 1.3% of ECG minutes (Gruwez et al., 2021).

## 1.2 Problem Statement

Every atrial ectopy threshold for selecting post-stroke monitoring was derived on Holter ECG, the resource the selection rations. The newest score still takes premature atrial contractions (PACs) from a single Holter (Maille et al., 2026), while scores that need no monitoring rank patients inconsistently across cohorts (Helbitz et al., 2025; Mele et al., 2025).

Beat intervals carry ectopy: PACs are the most common cause of false AF detections on implantable monitors (Mittal et al., 2021), and age with heart rate variability (HRV) from the first hour of stroke-unit ECG predicted AF with an external AUROC of 0.79 against 0.68 for the AS5F score, although that pipeline interpolated over ectopic beats (Schoels et al., 2025). Wearable ectopy performance is reported only as the classification of short windows, with PACs and premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) merged (Han et al., 2026). In its 72 stroke or TIA survivors, PACs and PVCs occupied 11.60% of clean PPG segments, while overall AF burden was 1.68% on PPG against 3.60% on the reference ECG, mainly because fewer PPG segments were clean and the watch recorded intermittently. The error of a derived daily ectopic-beat count, and the loss incurred when pulse morphology is removed and flagged beats are discarded, have not been quantified. Preliminary analysis (Section 4.2) indicates that the filtering loss is large and depends on ectopy burden itself.

Smartwatch observation is also informative rather than random: analysable PPG is night-weighted and falls with activity, while PACs peak in the afternoon and rise with daily activity (Johnson et al., 2021; Larsen et al., 2016; Merschel & Reinhardt, 2022).

Consequently, a smartwatch-derived ectopy value cannot yet be compared with ECG-derived thresholds or used for selection. This study therefore quantifies the information lost from PPG waveform to filtered beat intervals, develops a two-scale spatio-temporal graph neural network (ST-GNN) that estimates the ectopy rate using quality flags and unobserved time as input, and evaluates its deployability and selection value after stroke.

## 1.5 Significance of the Study

**Clinical.** Monitoring capacity is allocated by unstable scores or by an ectopy count that itself consumes monitoring; an estimate from a device the patient already wears breaks that dependency.

**Methodological and measurement.** Treating the quality flag as evidence rather than a deletion rule addresses a frequent flaw in prediction studies (Andaur Navarro et al., 2021), and an error-characterised daily count is needed before any ECG threshold can be transferred (Section 2.8). A coverage gate makes the estimator state when it cannot report a value, a prerequisite for clinical use of consumer wearable data.

# CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW

## 2.1 Positioning: From Static Recurrence Prediction to Signal-Based Monitoring

This proposal builds on the candidate's systematic review of machine-learning studies in stroke or TIA survivors (Amin et al., 2026). Of 69 studies from 62 cohorts, 42 predicted recurrence, and only one did so from a physiological signal: an in-hospital composite-outcome model evaluated on monitoring windows. The other 17 signal-based studies predicted AF or atrial ectopy. None used anomaly detection or a patient-specific baseline, four of the 42 recurrence models were externally validated, and only two of the 18 signal studies quantified how much data was lost to poor quality.

The review arranged the missing evidence as a staged framework from acquisition and signal-quality gating to a temporal model and calibrated risk. This study delivers the first stages for one driver: smartwatch beat intervals, signal quality as an input, and a daily ectopy rate.

## 2.2 Atrial Ectopy as a Selector for Prolonged Monitoring

The ectopy and AF association is consistent across populations (Binici et al., 2010; Larsen et al., 2015) and in cryptogenic stroke (Gladstone et al., 2015). A meta-analysis of 12 stroke studies (2,340 patients) found a pooled odds ratio of 4.16 (Tao et al., 2021), and more than 500 PACs per 24 hours carried an adjusted HR of 3.23 for monitor-detected AF (Shimada et al., 2024).

Ectopy has accordingly entered risk scores. PROACTIA retained PACs per 24 hours alongside P-wave and left atrial measures (Skrebelyte-Strøm et al., 2022). The PAVA score assigns two points to more than 200 PACs per 24 hours (c-index 0.79 in internal validation; Maille et al., 2026). In 221 patients with embolic stroke of undetermined source (ESUS), 24-hour atrial ectopic burden alone discriminated AF (AUROC 0.807), with an optimal cut-off of 0.050% (Jeon et al., 2023); this defines the range a wearable estimate must resolve. Each of these measures, however, is taken from a single Holter recording (Table A.1).


## 2.3 Clinical Risk Scores as the Alternative Selector

Clinical scores need no monitoring but rank patients unstably. A systematic review of 17 integer-based scores across 23 cohorts reported AUROC from 0.54 to 0.94 (Kishore et al., 2022). In cryptogenic stroke cohorts, the best score changed from study to study, and one group concluded that no score was adequate (Mele et al., 2025; Palaiodimou et al., 2024; Pinnacchio et al., 2026). A meta-analysis of 58 models in 75 studies rated 66% at high risk of bias, found no model excellent on external validation, and identified no clinical impact studies (Helbitz et al., 2025).

## 2.4 Ectopy Detection from Wearable Photoplethysmography

Ectopy disturbs wearable rhythm algorithms: PACs were the commonest cause of false AF on loop recorders (Mittal et al., 2021). Direct PPG ectopy detection has improved (Han et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022): on Pulsewatch, a recurrent network using PPG, accelerometry and heart rate reached PAC/PVC sensitivity of 83% (Han et al., 2026), while a densely connected network reached only 0.664, falling to 0.143 and 0.373 externally (Chen et al., 2024). Interval sequences alone carry most rhythm information: for AF, a recurrent model on heart-rate series reached AUROC 0.954 against 0.983 for the raw PPG waveform (Aschbacher et al., 2020). Two interval features separated PACs from PVCs at beat level on real PPG (F1 0.76; Vargova et al., 2026). As Table A.1 shows, all of this work classifies windows or beats; none reports a daily count.


## 2.5 Coverage, Timing and Informative Missingness

In a randomised trial of 120 stroke survivors aged 50 or older, the watch was worn on 21.2 of 30 days and daily wear fell from 73% to 63% over the month (Ding et al., 2023). After cryptogenic stroke, a median of 38% of smartwatch readings were of insufficient quality, and unconfirmed PPG AF corresponded to noise or premature beats (Huntelaar et al., 2024); valid PPG covered 62.5% of recording time against 96.2% for a patch ECG (Zuo et al., 2026).

The timing of this loss is not random. Analysable smartwatch PPG averaged 71.5% over 24 hours but exceeded 94% between midnight and 6 a.m., correlating with activity at r = −0.64 (Merschel & Reinhardt, 2022). PACs follow the opposite pattern, lowest around 6 a.m. and highest around 3 p.m. in people with frequent ectopy (Larsen et al., 2016). Day-to-day variation compounds this: after three days, daily PAC frequency differed by at least 50% from the 14-day mean in 25% of patients (Måneheim et al., 2024). An estimate that scales analysable hours to a day will therefore be biased low, and most so on active days and in high-burden patients.

## 2.6 Beat-Level Models, Graph Representations and Missing Data

Beat classifiers assume complete, trusted sequences (Hu et al., 2022), and supraventricular beats remain the weakest class: PAC sensitivity ranged from 0.539 to 0.797 even with P waves (Kraft et al., 2026), and a supraventricular positive predictive value (PPV) of 36.8% would inflate any daily count (Dias et al., 2021). Graph structure has not consistently helped: on visibility-graph features of single-lead ECG beats, a multilayer perceptron slightly outperformed a graph convolutional network (EPMoghaddam et al., 2024).

Most machine-learning prediction studies that report missing data use deletion (Nijman et al., 2022), and 41% of analyses handled it inadequately (Andaur Navarro et al., 2021). Methods that treat missingness as input perform better: masking and time since the last observation improved recurrent models over imputation (Che et al., 2018), although missing indicators can harm under outcome-dependent missingness (Sisk et al., 2023). An imperfect beat detector can still yield a usable daily rate: an implanted monitor with PVC beat sensitivity of 80.1% estimated 24-hour PVC burden with r = 0.95 against Holter (Siejko et al., 2025).

The contributions are of three types. Methodologically, the study treats the quality flag as evidence: a frozen retention model corrects for beats the device never reports, and a coverage gate decides when a daily value can be reported (Section 3.4, Equations 3.1 to 3.4). ## 3.3 Phase 1: Investigating the Information Loss (RO1)

Beat intervals discard the P wave that separates PACs from other premature beats. RO1 decomposes the resulting loss into three parts. RQ1.1 compares a waveform model with a beat-interval model on identical Pulsewatch recordings, attributing the performance difference to absent morphology. As Pulsewatch labels merge PACs and PVCs in 30-second windows, RQ1.1 measures premature-beat loss; PAC-specific loss is measured on Icentia11k. RQ1.2 applies deployed rejection rules, a percent-deviation filter (Karlsson et al., 2012) and an adaptive threshold (Lipponen & Tarvainen, 2019), to Icentia11k and records the share of annotated ectopic and normal beats removed at each threshold, producing a retention curve. RQ1.3 compares an origin-agnostic premature-beat count with the PAC-only count, stratified by ectopy burden. First results for RQ1.2 are reported in Section 4.2.

## 3.4 Phase 2: The Two-Scale ST-GNN (RO2)

The estimator operates at two scales (Figure 3.2). At the beat scale, the interval stream is split into windows; each window carries a banded temporal-chain graph and a k-nearest-neighbour similarity graph, combined in a gated local block. At the epoch scale, an interval block of graph convolution and a selective state-space layer operates across windows to aggregate the day. As Icentia11k segments are untimed, it trains there on pseudo-days built from each patient's segments, learning aggregation under gaps rather than a diurnal pattern; RQ2.2 is judged against the patient-level rate, and on Pulsewatch windows in real order across days, timed by file-name local time. An event head gives the beat PAC probabilities used in Equations 3.2 to 3.4; a rate head, an auxiliary target for the window PAC count, is not used in reported estimates.

The per-beat quality flag is treated as evidence rather than a deletion rule. The Garmin flag is binary, and it marks both motion-affected beats and any interval the device judges abnormally short or long (Garmin, 2023); a premature beat therefore tends to be flagged as low confidence. The flag is therefore both a quality and a weak ectopy signal, separated using the accelerometer (low-confidence beats at low motion are candidate rhythm events). The flag enters the model as an input, and a separate reliability pathway bypasses both encoders. As the Garmin flags abnormal beats and marks a gap where it gives none (Garmin, 2023), gaps enter the observed time and the pathway corrects only silent loss: retention (Equation 3.1) depends on motion and is estimated on Pulsewatch, which pairs wrist PPG and accelerometer magnitude with reference ECG R-peaks, outside stretches without a detected peak that mirror the Garmin gap rule. A premature beat with no wrist pulse leaves no reported beat to reweight, so pulse deficit is quantified as a bias in RO4a, not corrected. The flag adds no weight, so no beat is corrected twice. A per-session lag corrects the released alignment, which is not beat-accurate; a lag method not needing labelled segments is developed in RO2. A pre-specified check in 8 subjects found beat matching above chance at high motion and falling as motion rose, but absolute retention depended on the peak detector (0.850 vs 0.752), so the estimate will be reported with a detector sensitivity analysis. The primary detector implements Elgendi et al. (2013) on all segments, with the authors' released peaks as the alternative; transfer from Samsung to Garmin signals is a stated assumption. Retention is frozen and reweights reported beats (Equation 3.2); time of day acts only through motion and coverage. Because Equation 3.2 sums probabilities, p̂ᵢ is calibrated on validation subjects by temperature scaling (Guo et al., 2017). Icentia11k has no device flag, so training flags are simulated from this specification and the RO1 results, a stated assumption. A coverage gate, a fixed reporting rule rather than a learned layer, decides whether a daily value is reported at all (Equation 3.3).

Formally, for day d and time-of-day block h, let A(d,h) be the beats the device reports, π̂(m) the probability that a beat outside a gap is reported at motion m, p̂ᵢ the calibrated probability that beat i is a PAC, mᵢ its motion, and τ(d,h) the observed hours in the block. The retention model, daily rate, coverage gate and participant-level rate are:

EQ|π̂(m) = P(beat reported | motion m, outside a gap)|3.1
EQ|R̂(d) = Σₕ (Lₕ / 24) · [ Σᵢ∈A(d,h) p̂ᵢ / π̂(mᵢ) ] / τ(d,h)|3.2
EQ|report R̂(d) only if c(d, h) ≥ θ_block for every block h|3.3
EQ|R̄ = Σₕ (Lₕ / 24) · [ Σ_d∈V Σᵢ∈A(d,h) p̂ᵢ / π̂(mᵢ) ] / Σ_d∈V τ(d,h)|3.4

Here Lₕ = 6 hours is the length of block h (00–06, 06–12, 12–18 and 18–24 h), so the daily count 24 · R̂(d) weights each block by the time it covers, without an external diurnal profile; c(d, h) is the analysable coverage of block h, and θ_block is fixed in the registered protocol after device verification. Per-day values (Equations 3.2 and 3.3) serve day-to-day stability. ESVEA status uses the participant-level count 24 · R̄ (Equation 3.4), which pools each block across the set V of valid days and requires at least 1 analysable hour per block across the week, so a block thin on one day is informed by the same block on other days. In a sensitivity analysis, block rates are instead shrunk towards a published diurnal profile (relative rates 0.87, 0.63, 1.11 and 1.40, read from the figure of median hourly PACs in 66 participants with frequent PACs; Larsen et al., 2016) with strength λ = 1 hour, with 0.5, 2 and 4 hours also reported. A calibrated simulation (Appendix C) found pooling as accurate as shrinkage and better than day-restricted averaging under poor coverage; λ changed ESVEA class in at most 3% of participants.

## 3.5 Phase 3: Deployability Study (RO3)

Eligible participants are adults after ischaemic stroke or TIA without known AF, approached consecutively after discharge at Pusat Perubatan Universiti Malaya (PPUM; University of Malaya Medical Centre). The device has not yet been received, so assumptions follow the published specification (Garmin, 2023), which describes overnight intervals, and Garmin's confirmation of 24-hour logging. Before ethics submission, a device verification step on non-participant wear will confirm export, flag and gap encoding, 24-hour interval logging and 7-day battery life, and set the day, night and per-block coverage thresholds. Seven days were chosen because sensitivity for a high daily PAC frequency rises from 76.6% on one day to 88.8% after seven (Måneheim et al., 2024), matching the MonDAFIS Holter duration (Haeusler et al., 2021). Valid days need coverage in both a day and a night block because analysable time is night-weighted while PACs are afternoon-weighted (Larsen et al., 2016; Merschel & Reinhardt, 2022). A valid day counts towards the primary outcome, but a daily count and ESVEA class are reported only when every 6-hour block also meets the per-block minimum (Equation 3.3); the share of valid days meeting it answers RQ3.2. Computability of the pooled count (Equation 3.4) is a key secondary outcome (Appendix C).

The sample size follows the traffic-light method for binary feasibility outcomes. Testing against an upper red boundary of 50% with an expected green value of 75%, at 90% power and one-sided α of 0.05, requires about 34 analysable participants (Lewis et al., 2021). Recruiting about 40 allows for withdrawal (Tse et al., 2024). Secondary questions cover yield (RQ3.1), computability of the daily ESVEA denominator (RQ3.2), day-to-day stability (RQ3.3), activity, including whether the flagged-beat fraction rises with motion as on Pulsewatch (RQ3.4), and the ESVEA proportion as a plausibility check (Haeusler et al., 2025; Teare et al., 2014) (RQ3.5).

## 3.6 Phase 4: Substitution and Validation (RO4)

RO4a tests substitution where ECG truth exists. Reference ESVEA status is computed from the patient-level rate in Icentia11k patients held out from RO1 calibration and RO2 training; their records are degraded to smartwatch-like intervals and re-estimated by the frozen RO2 model. Four degradation operators are applied, each calibrated from measured data (Table 3.4) and sampled independently of the training simulation, and results are reported across a severity sweep for each operator. Such simulation has precedent (Aguilar et al., 2026) but is likely optimistic, because Icentia11k intervals are ECG RR rather than PPG, and Garmin PPG agrees only moderately with ECG for short-term variability after stroke (Theurl et al., 2023).

W|20|40|40
Operator | What it simulates | Calibrated from

ENDTABLE

No PAC-specific missed-pulse rate has been published (Zheng et al., 2008), so the pulse-deficit threshold is swept. Outcomes are the reclassification rate of ESVEA status, and κ, sensitivity and specificity at the ESVEA cut-off. Preliminary analysis found 67 of 298 Icentia11k patients at or above 480 per day (54 at 30 or more per hour; Section 4.2), so a held-out sample of similar size estimates sensitivity at the cut-off within about ±8 percentage points.

RO4b compares wearable-based selection with CHA2DS2-VASc, HAVOC, C2HEST, AS5F and Brown ESUS-AF (ESUS subgroup only; Kishore et al., 2022) at equal monitoring capacity, meaning each method selects the same number of participants as the wearable classifies ESVEA-positive, in the RO3 cohort, reporting Cohen's κ, Jaccard overlap and the profile of discordant patients. Net reclassification improvement needs observed events (Pencina et al., 2014), and fewer than five AF events are expected, so RO4b is descriptive.

Each model is trained with five random seeds, and sensitivity to window length and graph neighbourhood size is reported. ## 3.8 Ethical Considerations

Medical research ethics approval will be obtained before recruitment, from the PPUM Medical Research Ethics Committee. Participants will give written informed consent. Data will be pseudonymised at collection, stored on university infrastructure in line with the Personal Data Protection Act 2010. Because watch data are not reviewed in real time, participants are told that the study does not provide rhythm monitoring for clinical care, and any rhythm concern raised during the study is referred through usual care. Device data are exported under study-managed, pseudonymous accounts rather than participants' personal accounts where possible. Any use of generative artificial intelligence tools in preparing the report will be declared as required by university policy (Universiti Malaya, 2025).

## 4.2 Information Loss from Interval Filtering (RO1 and RO4a)

A first RQ1.2 analysis used beat annotations from 300 randomly sampled Icentia11k patients (fixed seed); 298 were analysed, with a median of 57.5 hours (interquartile range 55.5 to 58.1) each. Unclassifiable beats and intervals touching them were excluded. A filter rejected any interval deviating from the median of the five preceding accepted intervals by more than 10% to 30%, and removals were recorded by adjacent beat class (Table 4.2).

At the commonly used 20% threshold, the filter removed 60.9% of PAC coupling intervals and 54.5% of the pauses that follow them, about seven times the 8.8% of normal intervals removed. The loss also varied with burden: 78.5% of PAC coupling intervals were removed in ESVEA-negative patients against 60.0% in ESVEA-positive patients, probably because frequent PACs enter the running reference and are then accepted. A single correction factor therefore cannot restore the count after deletion, which supports keeping flagged beats as input (RQ2.4).

# CHAPTER 5: EXPECTED RESEARCH OUTCOMES AND CONCLUSION

## 5.1 Expected Research Outcomes

RO1 is expected to quantify, to our knowledge for the first time, how much ectopy information is lost to absent morphology and to filtering. RO2 is expected to produce a coverage-aware two-scale ST-GNN and an error profile for a daily ectopy count under simulated smartwatch losses. RO3 and RO4 are expected to show whether a 7-day protocol meets progression criteria, how often a wearable estimate reclassifies ESVEA under device losses, and how its selections differ from clinical scores. If H2 is rejected, the retention correction and coverage gate are still evaluated on the best baseline, RO1, RO3 and RO4a remain informative, and the negative result is reported as evidence for simpler models.

## 5.2 Conclusion

The clinical decision exists and atrial ectopy addresses it, but the measurement is unvalidated on wearable intervals, its delivery is untested in post-stroke users, and no method treats the observation process as part of estimation. The four objectives follow in that order, the validation design states where ground truth exists for each, and the pilot work indicates that the representation is feasible.

# APPENDIX C: SIMULATION FOR THE PARTICIPANT-LEVEL ESTIMATE

A pre-specified simulation generated seven days of four 6-hour blocks per participant to compare the per-day estimate, the pooled participant-level estimate (Equation 3.4) and shrinkage towards the published diurnal profile, before real data exist. Each layer is calibrated to a published value or swept where unknown (Table C.1). Retention and PAC identification were treated as known, so the simulation isolates coverage. Beat loss was set to the 20% filter retention of 0.391, which is pessimistic for a device that flags abnormal beats rather than deleting them (Garmin, 2023); results therefore isolate coverage under heavy beat loss.

TABLE|Table C.1: Simulation layers, sources and calibration
W|24|46|30

Diurnal pattern | Block shape 0.87, 0.63, 1.11, 1.40 (Larsen et al., 2016, Figure 3); individual deviation swept | Swept
Day-to-day variation | Calibrated to 76.6% one-day and 88.8% seven-day sensitivity (Måneheim et al., 2024) | 76.0% to 77.7%; 88.3% to 89.4%

With typical coverage, misclassification at 480 per day was 2.1% to 2.5% for the per-day estimate and 1.8% to 2.1% for pooling; with poor coverage, 4.2% to 4.4% against 2.7% to 3.3%, with median count error falling from about 20% to about 14%. Varying λ from 0.5 to 4 hours changed ESVEA class in 0.7% to 3.0% of participants. Under poor coverage, 58% to 61% of simulated participants had three or more day/night-valid days, but only 20% to 21% had three or more days meeting the per-block rule; the pooled count remained computable in 87% to 88%.
